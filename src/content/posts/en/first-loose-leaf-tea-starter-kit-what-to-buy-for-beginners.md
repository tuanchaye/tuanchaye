A workable first loose leaf kit is smaller and cheaper than the internet suggests. Buy three teas at 50 g each — a black, a green, and a light oolong — one vessel big enough for the leaves to open, a basket infuser that fills it, and a kitchen scale that reads to a tenth of a gram. That is the whole kit. The pot, the kettle, the timer, and the storage tin can be solved with what you already own, and most of what gets sold as a beginner set is either too small to brew with or too much to finish before it goes stale.

Everything below explains the choices in that table, and the last section covers what to leave on the shelf.

## The Three Teas, and Why Those Three

The point of buying three is coverage. Tea styles are separated by how much oxidation the leaf goes through, and three teas placed at different points on that scale teach your palate more than ten samples of the same type. They also happen to be the three most forgiving styles to brew badly.

| Style                                                        | What to look for                               | Water     | Time        |
| ------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------- | --------- | ----------- |
| Black — Assam or Ceylon                                      | Whole or broken leaf, not CTC dust             | 95–100 °C | 3–4 minutes |
| Green — Chinese pan-fired or Japanese sencha                 | Bright color, fresh grassy smell, no yellowing | 70–80 °C  | 2–3 minutes |
| Light oolong — Tieguanyin style or a balled Taiwanese oolong | Tightly rolled leaf, floral smell              | 85–90 °C  | 3 minutes   |

Fifty grams is roughly fifteen to twenty mugs at the three-gram dose below, which is enough to learn a tea and not so much that you are still drinking it next year. [What separates a good loose leaf from a mediocre one](<>) is mostly freshness and leaf integrity rather than price, and at this stage it is the only quality signal worth paying attention to.

**Leave these for later.** White tea is expensive, delicate, and easy to over-steep while you are still learning. Pu-erh is compressed, often an acquired taste, and needs a different brewing approach. Flavored and smoked blends hide the leaf, which defeats the purpose of buying leaf. Matcha needs a whisk and a technique of its own. Single-estate premium lots are wasted money before your palate has anything to compare them against.

## The Vessel: Room Matters More Than Material

Whole leaves expand to several times their dry size. A ball infuser gives them nowhere to go, so the water cannot circulate and you get a thin, uneven cup from perfectly good tea. This is the single most common reason people conclude that loose leaf is not worth the trouble.

A 300 to 400 ml pot, or a 250 to 300 ml mug with a basket that nearly fills it, leaves enough space for a three-gram dose to open fully. Porcelain holds heat better than glass; glass lets you watch the leaves move. Either is fine, and the difference between them matters far less than the size.

Skip the gaiwan for now. It is a brewing technique rather than a beginner's tool, and learning it while also learning what tea you like is two problems at once.

## A Scale Does More for Your Tea Than a Kettle

Start at one gram of leaf per 100 ml of water — three grams in a 300 ml mug. Adjust from there, but start with a number, because the usual instruction to use "one teaspoon per cup" is close to useless. A teaspoon of tightly rolled oolong weighs around four grams; a teaspoon of large, fluffy white leaf weighs about one. Same spoon, four times the tea.

A ten-dollar scale settles that. A hundred-dollar kettle with one-degree control does not, because the useful temperature range for green tea is wide enough that "hot but not boiling" gets you most of the way there. If you want to be precise without the expense, a cheap thermometer in a plain kettle does the same job. [Our guide to tea-to-water ratios](<>) goes further into doses by style once you have the basics down.

For black tea, the widely quoted guidance is three to five minutes in freshly boiled water; the lower end of that range suits smaller-leaf teas, which give up their flavor faster.

## Water

Use fresh water and do not re-boil what is already in the kettle. Repeated boiling drives off dissolved oxygen and flattens the taste, which is a real effect and an easy one to test for yourself.

If your tap water tastes strongly of chlorine or is very hard, filter it — a plain carbon filter is enough. Do not buy bottled water for tea. Water chemistry does change what gets extracted from the leaf, but the variable that decides whether the cup tastes good at home is whether the water tastes good on its own, and that is a question you can answer with a glass.

## How Much to Buy, and How to Keep It

Fifty grams per tea is the right starting quantity. Buying 500 g of anything before you know you like it is how people end up with stale tea and the impression that loose leaf is disappointing.

Store each tea in its own airtight tin, away from light, heat, and moisture. Not the refrigerator: every time you open the tin, cold tea meets warm air and collects condensation, which is exactly what you are trying to avoid. Buy from a shop that prints a harvest or packing date on the packet, because for tea that date is more informative than any description on the front. [How long tea keeps and how to store it](<>) covers the timelines by type.

## What Not to Buy

**Sampler boxes.** Ten tins of five grams each look like good value and are not. Five grams is two cups — not enough to learn a tea, and often not enough to brew it twice and compare. The leaf is also usually the cheapest the seller has.

**Ball infusers and novelty infusers.** Too small for whole leaf, whatever shape they come in.

**Silicone baskets.** They hold on to the smell of whatever was brewed last.

**A gaiwan or a yixing pot.** Both are good objects. Neither is a beginner purchase. A yixing pot also commits you to one tea type, since the clay takes on the flavor of whatever goes in it.

**Tea baggers.** You are moving to loose leaf partly to get away from the fannings and dust in commercial bags. Re-bagging whole leaf into paper pouches undoes that and adds a step.

**Plastic mesh infusers.** A 2019 study in *Environmental Science & Technology* found that plastic teabags steeped at brewing temperature released billions of microplastic and nanoplastic particles into the water. That study measured what comes off the plastic, not what it does in the body, so treat it as a reason to prefer metal or paper filtration rather than a health claim. Metal or paper is also simply better at the job.

## What It Actually Costs

The teas should be the largest line in the budget, and the equipment should be the smallest. As a rough structure at entry level: three 50 g packets will usually cost more than the vessel, and the scale should be the cheapest thing you buy. If you find yourself spending more on the pot than on the tea, you have the ratio backwards.

Nothing in this list needs to be bought new. A plain porcelain pot from a charity shop, a mug you already own, and a kitchen scale that lives in a drawer will brew as well as anything marketed as a tea set.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need a kettle with temperature settings?

No, but it helps if you drink green tea. Boiled water left to stand for a few minutes drops into the right range, and a cheap thermometer removes the guesswork. One-degree control is a convenience, not a requirement.

### How much tea should I buy to start?

Fifty grams of each of three styles. That is roughly fifteen to twenty mugs per tea at three grams a cup — enough to learn the tea, small enough that you will finish it while it is still fresh.

### Is loose leaf more expensive than tea bags?

Per gram, yes. Per cup the gap narrows, because you are comparing whole leaf against fannings and dust, and because a three-gram dose of good leaf goes further than the equivalent weight of broken leaf. At the entry level the difference is smaller than the price tags suggest.

### If I only buy one tea, which should it be?

A black tea — Assam for malt and body, Ceylon for a brighter, cleaner cup. It tolerates a wide range of water temperatures and steep times without turning harsh, which makes it the most forgiving place to start.

## Key Takeaways

* A functional starter kit is three teas at 50 g, one vessel wide enough for the leaves to open, a basket infuser, and a scale.
* Room in the vessel matters more than the material, because whole leaves need space to unfurl or the cup comes out thin.
* Weigh the leaf instead of scooping it — a teaspoon of balled oolong can weigh four times a teaspoon of large white leaf.
* Start at one gram per 100 ml, and buy from a seller who prints a harvest or packing date.
* The teas should cost more than the equipment; if the pot costs more than the tea, the ratio is wrong.
