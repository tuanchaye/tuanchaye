At ordinary amounts, tea does not dehydrate you. Controlled trials comparing black tea with the same volume of water have found no meaningful difference in urine output, blood osmolality, or any other hydration marker. Caffeine is a mild diuretic, but the dose needed to produce a measurable fluid loss is far larger than what is in a mug of tea — and it mostly affects people who have not had caffeine in days.

The interesting question is not whether tea hydrates. It is where that stops being true.

## Why the Myth Exists

The mechanism behind the myth is real. Caffeine reduces sodium reabsorption in the kidney tubules, so more sodium leaves in urine and water follows it. That is a genuine diuretic effect, and it is why the advice to "drink a glass of water with your coffee" has circulated for decades.

What the advice leaves out is dose. A mug of tea contains roughly 40 to 50 mg of caffeine. A review by Maughan and Griffin found that a measurable short-term diuretic effect appears above roughly 250 to 300 mg taken at once, and mainly in people who had abstained from caffeine for days beforehand. That is five to eight mugs of tea in a single sitting, drunk by someone who does not normally consume caffeine. Habitual drinkers develop what the same review called a tolerance to the effect, so their usual intake produces no extra fluid loss at all.

## What the Trials Found

| Ruxton & Hart, 2011 (*Br J Nutr*)       | Crossover RCT, 21 men, 19 completed                       | 4 or 6 × 240 ml black tea (168 / 252 mg caffeine) vs the same volume of boiled water; blood at 0–12 h, 24-h urine | No significant difference on any blood or urine measure |
| Maughan et al., 2016 (*Am J Clin Nutr*) | RCT, 72 men, 1 L of 13 different drinks                   | Urine collected for 4 h; beverage hydration index                                                                 | Hot tea and iced tea both matched water                 |
| Zhang et al., 2015 (*J Sci Med Sport*)  | Meta-analysis, 16 studies, 379 people, median dose 300 mg | Effect size on urine volume                                                                                       | +109 ml (+16%) overall; effect absent during exercise   |
| Armstrong et al., 2005 (*IJSNEM*)       | 11 days, caffeine capsules at 0 / 3 / 6 mg per kg per day | 20 hydration markers, including 24-h urine and serum osmolality                                                   | No sign of underhydration at any dose                   |

The Ruxton trial is the most direct test of tea as people actually drink it. The tea was made from bags in a standardized way, brewed 40 seconds, with 20 ml of semi-skimmed milk added — an ordinary British cup, not a laboratory preparation. Even at six mugs, the highest intake tested, blood and urine measurements were indistinguishable from water.

The Maughan trial is worth reading past the headline. It tested 13 drinks and found that skim milk, whole milk, and oral rehydration solution were retained *better* than water, with hydration indices of 1.58, 1.50, and 1.54. Tea did not beat water. It only matched it. That distinction gets lost in most summaries.

## Where the Effect Is Real

The Zhang meta-analysis contains a detail that secondary coverage almost always drops. The overall effect size for caffeine-induced diuresis was 0.29 — small. But split by condition, it was 0.54 at rest, which is a moderate effect and statistically significant, and 0.10 during exercise, which is not significant at all. Exercise cancels it.

The same analysis found a sex difference: effect size 0.75 in women against 0.13 in men. The male result did not reach significance. The female result did.

So the accurate statement is narrower than "tea never dehydrates anyone." If you are sitting still, are female, and take a large caffeine dose in one go, the effect is measurable. In absolute terms it is still modest — an average of 109 ml of extra urine across the pooled studies — but "negligible" overstates how settled that is for everyone.

Temperature, at least, is settled. Hot and iced tea were tested separately in the Maughan trial, and neither differed from water.

## How Strong Is This Evidence?

Honest accounting matters more here than in most topics, because a lot of tea-and-hydration writing is funded by people who sell tea.

The Ruxton trial was funded by the Tea Advisory Panel, an industry body. The Armstrong trial was supported by the IAFNS Caffeine Working Group. That does not make the findings wrong — the designs were controlled, the outcomes were objective laboratory measures rather than self-report, and independent groups have reached the same conclusion — but you should know it when you weigh them.

The limitations are also real: 21 men in Ruxton, participants mostly young and mostly male, durations from a single day to eleven days. None of these trials followed people for months.

What makes the conclusion hold up is replication. Four separate research groups, using different designs, different doses, and different populations, all failed to find dehydration. That pattern is more persuasive than any single study in it.

## What Tea Cannot Do

Tea counts toward your fluid intake. It is not a rehydration fluid.

If you have been sweating heavily, the thing your body needs is sodium, and that is where the hydration index becomes useful: oral rehydration solution and milk retained substantially more fluid than water, and tea did not. Drinking tea after a long run in heat is fine. Treating it as recovery is not.

The reason to cap your tea intake is not water, it is caffeine. A mug has 40 to 50 mg, so six mugs is 240 to 300 mg — enough to matter if you are sensitive, and enough to disrupt sleep if you drink them late. Our guide to [tea and sleep](<>) covers the timing question in detail.

Two groups should be more careful. During pregnancy, caffeine is generally capped at 200 mg a day, which is roughly four mugs of tea — see [tea during pregnancy](<>) for how that adds up across tea, coffee, and chocolate. And if you take a diuretic for blood pressure or have an overactive bladder, the mild effect that is invisible in a healthy 25-year-old may be noticeable for you.

Sweetened or milk-heavy tea is a separate issue. Sugar and milk change the calorie load, not the hydration.

## How Much Fluid You Actually Need

Two reference points, both of which count all beverages, tea included.

Neither figure means "drink that much liquid." Roughly a fifth of total water intake normally comes from food. The practical reading is that tea sits in the same category as water in both frameworks, and neither body asks you to discount it.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does tea count toward my daily water intake?

Yes. Brewed tea is almost entirely water, and the caffeine in a normal serving is too small to offset the fluid it arrives in. Both the EFSA and the National Academies count it.

### How much tea would actually dehydrate me?

Roughly five to eight mugs at once — 250 to 300 mg of caffeine — and mainly if you have not had caffeine in recent days. Spread across a day at normal strength, it produces no net fluid loss.

### Is decaf tea more hydrating than regular tea?

Not meaningfully. Regular tea already matches water at ordinary intakes, so there is little room to improve. Decaf is a sensible choice in the evening or if you are caffeine-sensitive, but not because of hydration.

### Does adding milk or sugar change how hydrating tea is?

No. Milk and sugar change the calorie and nutrient content, not the fluid balance. Milk may actually slow fluid loss slightly, since dairy scored above water on the hydration index.

## Key Takeaways

* At normal servings, tea hydrates about as well as water — four controlled studies across different designs found no dehydration.
* Caffeine is a mild diuretic, but a measurable effect needs roughly 250 to 300 mg at once, which is five to eight mugs in a single sitting.
* The effect is real at rest (effect size 0.54) and absent during exercise (0.10), and it is larger in women than in men.
* Tea matches water; it does not beat it. Milk and oral rehydration solution retain more fluid and are the better choice after heavy sweating.
* The reason to moderate tea is caffeine and sleep, not hydration — and pregnancy caps caffeine at about 200 mg a day.
