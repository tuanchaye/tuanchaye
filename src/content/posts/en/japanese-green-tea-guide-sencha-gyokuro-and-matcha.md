Japanese green tea is steamed, not pan-fired, and that choice, made within hours of picking, is why the leaf stays green. Within that family, three names do most of the work. What separates them is not three different plants but two decisions made long before you open a tin: **how long the bushes were covered**, and **whether you drink the leaf or only its infusion**.

## One Plant, Two Decisions

Fresh tea leaves oxidize fast. Japanese processors stop that by steaming the leaf for roughly 15 to 60 seconds, which deactivates the enzymes before they can brown it. Chinese green teas usually take the other route and kill the enzymes in a hot wok. The steaming approach leaves a greener, more vegetal cup with a marine edge; pan-firing pushes toward chestnut and toast. The full sequence is covered in [how tea is made](<>).

The second decision is shade. About three weeks before harvest, growers cover the bushes with reed screens or black cloth and block out most of the light. Theanine, the amino acid behind tea's savory depth, is built in the roots and shipped to the young shoots; light drives its breakdown and drives catechin production. Cut the light and theanine accumulates while catechins fall, so umami rises and astringency drops.

Shading raises caffeine as well. A 2022 transcriptome study on matcha-destined plants found that 85% and 95% shade both increased theanine and caffeine while decreasing epicatechin and epigallocatechin. This is the fact most tea charts leave out, and it matters later.

## Sencha: The Everyday Leaf

Sencha is grown in full sun and is the largest single category of Japanese tea by volume, the tea most Japanese households actually drink. The dry leaf is a bright green needle. Brewed well it is grassy and clean with a light astringency that fades quickly.

![](https://s.coze.cn/image/PKsgWCou8Do/)

Sencha is also the tea most often ruined. Water at a rolling boil pulls out catechins and caffeine fast and turns sencha harsh and bitter. Aim for 70 to 80 °C: boil the water, pour it into your cups first, let it sit a minute, then pour it over the leaf. The mechanics of that cooling step are worth learning properly — see [water temperature for tea](<>).

## Gyokuro: Three Weeks Under Cover

Gyokuro is the shaded version, covered for about 20 to 30 days before the spring pick. The leaf comes out darker and glossier than sencha, almost blue-green, and the cup tastes less like "tea" than like a light broth — savory, thick, sweet, with very little bitterness.

Brew it cool and concentrated: 50 to 60 °C, a small volume of water, around two minutes. Traditional service uses tiny cups and only a few ounces at a time. This is not a tea for a mug, and treating it like one wastes both the leaf and the money.

## Matcha Is Ground Tencha, Not Ground Sencha

Matcha starts as tencha: shaded leaf that is steamed, then dried without being rolled, with stems and veins sifted out. The remaining flakes are stone-milled into a powder fine enough to suspend in water. Because you swallow the leaf rather than steeping and discarding it, everything in that leaf ends up in you.

That is why grade matters more than brand. Ceremonial grade comes from a first-harvest, well-shaded crop with the veins removed; it is sweet enough to drink with only hot water. Culinary grade uses later, less-shaded leaf and is deliberately more astringent — fine in a latte or a cake, unpleasant whisked straight. If a matcha tastes harsh and thin, that is usually the leaf, not your technique.

Whisking has its own grammar, covered in the [Japanese tea ceremony guide](<>). For everyday use, sift 1.5 to 2 g into a dry bowl, add 50 to 70 ml of water at 70 to 80 °C, and whisk in a fast W motion until a fine foam forms.

## The Caffeine Is Not Where You Expect It

Most people assume the gentle, expensive tea is the mild one. Per gram of dry leaf, gyokuro carries roughly 35 mg of caffeine against about 25 mg for standard sencha — close to 40% more. Shading did that — the same mechanism that built the umami.

Cool water partly hides that. Amino acids dissolve readily at almost any temperature, while catechins and caffeine both need heat. Brew gyokuro at 55 °C and you hold back most of the bitterness — but you are still extracting from a leaf that started with more caffeine than sencha. A smooth, savory cup is not a weak cup.

Matcha is the other extreme, because the whole leaf is consumed: a 2 g serving lands around 60 to 80 mg of caffeine, roughly a cup of coffee, in a bowl you can finish in three sips. Hojicha sits at the opposite end, but not for the reason usually given. Roasting does drive some caffeine off — caffeine sublimes at around 178 °C and hojicha is roasted hotter than that — yet the bigger factor is the raw material. Hojicha is built from mature late-season leaves and stems, which never held much caffeine to begin with. If you are counting milligrams, read the base leaf, not the roast color. ## The Water Matters More Than the Vessel

Hard water dulls Japanese green tea. Calcium and magnesium bind the polyphenols and amino acids that carry flavor, and the bicarbonate that often comes with hardness pushes the liquor toward flat and chalky. Very soft water can taste thin; moderately soft filtered water is the practical middle. Skip strongly mineral bottled waters, and skip anything alkaline.

This is also why the cooling step matters more than the pot. A clay kyusu is pleasant, but it will not rescue leaf that was hit with boiling water.

## Beyond the Big Three

Kabusecha is shaded for only a week or two, landing between sencha and gyokuro in both price and character. Bancha is the coarse late-season pick, cheap and sturdy. Genmaicha blends bancha or sencha with toasted rice, which softens the cup and adds a nutty note. Hojicha is roasted — brown leaf, toasty aroma, almost no bitterness, and the most forgiving Japanese tea to brew badly. Shincha is simply the first flush of the year, sold young and drunk fresh.

## What the Longevity Research Actually Shows

The most-cited Japanese cohort is the Ohsaki study, which followed 40,530 adults aged 40 to 79 for up to 11 years. Compared with people drinking less than one cup of green tea a day, those drinking five or more had a hazard ratio of 0.84 for all-cause mortality — a 16% lower rate — with a stronger association in women (0.77) than men (0.88). For cardiovascular death the association was larger, with women at 0.69.

Two things get dropped when this study is quoted. First, there was **no significant association with cancer mortality** in any consumption category. Second, this is an observational cohort: it can show that tea drinkers died less often, not that tea caused it, and heavy green tea intake in Japan travels with a whole diet and lifestyle. A hazard ratio of 0.84 is not a prescription to drink five cups.

## Where It Grows

Shizuoka is the largest producing prefecture, followed by Kagoshima and Mie. Uji, in Kyoto, is the historical center for gyokuro and matcha, grown in a misty river valley where fog and cool nights slow the leaf. Yame in Fukuoka and Nishio in Aichi are the other names worth knowing on a matcha tin.

## Storing It

Green tea is not a long-ager. Light, heat, moisture and odor all strip it within months. Keep it airtight, cool and dark, and drink sencha within a year of harvest. Matcha is more fragile still — refrigerate it sealed, and let the tin reach room temperature before opening so condensation does not form inside.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is gyokuro or sencha higher in caffeine?

Gyokuro, by a clear margin. Shading raises caffeine along with theanine, so gyokuro's dry leaf carries roughly 40% more caffeine per gram than sencha, and it is brewed with far more leaf per unit of water. The smooth, savory flavor makes it feel gentler than it is.

### Can I drink culinary-grade matcha?

You can, but you probably will not want to. Culinary grade is made from later, less-shaded leaf and is intentionally more bitter and astringent — it is designed to survive being mixed into milk, sugar and batter. Whisked with plain water it tastes harsh.

### Does Japanese green tea have more caffeine than coffee?

Usually less, cup for cup. A cup of sencha lands well below a cup of drip coffee, while a 2 g bowl of matcha is closer to coffee territory. The variable that trips people up is serving size, not the tea itself.

### What is the difference between sencha and kabusecha?

Shade. Kabusecha is covered for one to two weeks before harvest, while sencha grows in full sun. That short cover is enough to lift theanine and soften astringency, which is why kabusecha sits between sencha and gyokuro in flavor and price.

## Key Takeaways

* Japanese green tea is defined by steaming, which stops oxidation within hours of picking and keeps the leaf green.
* Sencha is the full-sun everyday tea; gyokuro and matcha come from bushes shaded for about three weeks, which raises umami and lowers astringency.
* Matcha is ground tencha — the whole leaf, consumed. Grade determines whether it is drinkable with water alone.
* Shading raises caffeine as well as theanine, so gyokuro is stronger than it tastes. Matcha delivers the most per serving.
* Brew cool: 70–80 °C for sencha, 50–60 °C for gyokuro. Boiling water is what makes green tea bitter.
