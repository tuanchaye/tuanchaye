The short answer is a number: **200 milligrams of caffeine a day**. That is the ceiling used by the NHS, ACOG and the European Food Safety Authority — roughly two to three mugs of ordinary tea.

But caffeine is only the part everyone talks about. Three things matter as much and get far less airtime: tea with meals blocks some of the iron in plant foods; licorice root should be avoided outright, not merely limited; and the herbal tea most often recommended in late pregnancy has reassuring but thin evidence behind it.

## The Number That Matters: 200 mg a Day

The NHS tells pregnant women not to exceed 200 mg of caffeine a day, and notes that consistently going over has been linked to a higher chance of miscarriage and stillbirth. ACOG puts the same threshold more carefully: moderate consumption, under 200 mg a day, "does not appear to be a major contributing factor in miscarriage or preterm birth" — while adding that the relationship to fetal growth restriction "remains undetermined."

That last clause matters. The 200 mg figure is a level at which harm has not been demonstrated, not one proven safe.

**The mug trap.** The NHS puts a cup of tea at about 75 mg; the USDA figure for 8 oz is 47 mg. Both are right: a British mug holds closer to 350 ml and is usually brewed strong, a US cup is 237 ml. If you drink out of a big mug, count it as two — the most common way women overshoot 200 mg.

**The second steep.** Most of the caffeine leaves the leaf in the first infusion. Brew the same leaves again and you get roughly half, and roughly half again on the third. If you want the ritual more than the stimulant, resteeping is the easiest lever you have.

**The rest of the budget.** Tea is rarely the only source. By NHS figures, a can of cola is about 40 mg, a 50 g bar of dark chocolate under 25 mg, a mug of filter coffee around 140 mg — two of those would put you over the limit before a single cup of tea. Guarana and some "energy" supplements are caffeine in disguise.

## Tea and Iron: The Rule That Matters More Than the Caffeine Math

Tea's polyphenols — the compounds behind its bitterness — bind non-heme iron, the form found in plants, beans, lentils and fortified cereals. Drink strong tea with an iron-rich meal and you absorb less of that iron.

In pregnancy this is not a footnote. Blood volume rises by roughly 40 to 50 percent and iron demand with it; iron-deficiency anemia is among the most common complications of pregnancy. A habit of tea with every meal works against you in a way no caffeine chart will show.

The fix is timing, not abstinence. Leave an hour between a cup of tea and an iron-rich meal or your prenatal supplement. If you do drink tea with food, include a vitamin C source in the same meal — citrus, peppers, tomatoes — which offsets the inhibition. And take iron tablets with water or juice, never with tea.

## Green Tea, Folic Acid, and a Test-Tube Finding

Search "green tea pregnancy" and you will meet a warning that EGCG, green tea's main catechin, inhibits dihydrofolate reductase — the enzyme that recycles folate. The finding is real, but it comes from in-vitro work.

Enzyme inhibition in a dish is not folate deficiency in a person drinking two cups. No study has shown that ordinary green tea causes neural tube defects, and a case-control analysis of spina bifida found no overall association with tea, though it flagged a possible interaction at higher intakes.

The practical conclusion is unglamorous: keep green tea moderate, and take your folic acid supplement as prescribed. It is the one supplement where the evidence for neural tube protection is strong, and not something to leave to a beverage.

## Herbal Tea Is Not Automatically Safe

"Herbal" means "not from *Camellia sinensis*" — [a distinction worth understanding](<>). It does not mean "studied in pregnancy." Most herbs in infusions have never been tested in pregnant women, which is why the NHS caps herbal tea at four cups a day and advises against anything containing licorice root.

![](https://s.coze.cn/image/MfbcI7FwseU/)

Licorice is the one clear prohibition. Licorice root contains glycyrrhizin, and high intakes have been linked to harm to the baby's development — enough that the NHS lists licorice-root tea as a drink to avoid, not limit. Licorice sweets are fine in normal amounts; licorice *root* tea is not.

Beyond that, the avoid list is pharmacology rather than folklore:

* **Laxative and "detox" teas** — senna, cascara, and anything sold as slimming or cleansing. These can genuinely cause harm, and are rarely marketed as pregnancy products at all.
* **Pennyroyal, comfrey, coltsfoot** — traditional herbs with documented toxicity.
* **Sage in quantity** — fine as a seasoning, not as a daily infusion.
* **Dandelion and nettle** — diuretics, the opposite of useful when fluid needs are up.
* **Multi-herb "pregnancy tea" blends** — eight herbs in one bag means eight unknowns and no way to attribute an effect.

Two herbs are generally acceptable in normal amounts: **ginger** for nausea and **peppermint** for heartburn — though peppermint relaxes the esophageal sphincter and worsens reflux for some women. Chamomile is likewise fine in moderation.

## Raspberry Leaf: What the Evidence Actually Says

This is the herb you will be told to buy, so it deserves a straight answer.

Raspberry leaf is used by roughly a quarter of pregnant women in the UK and over a third in Australia, almost always in the belief that it tones the uterus and shortens labor. **No randomized trial has shown that it shortens labor** — that is where the reviews land.

On safety, the human data are reassuring but thin. A retrospective cohort of 57 users and 51 controls found no adverse effects, and a placebo-controlled trial in 192 first-time mothers taking 2.4 g of extract daily from 32 weeks found none either, apart from possible constipation in four women. The UK Committee on Toxicity reviewed all of it in 2024 and concluded the risk was **low, but with high uncertainty** because the data are so limited. The European Medicines Agency went further in 2013: raspberry leaf could not be recommended in pregnancy, as efficacy and safety evidence was lacking.

Two caveats. UK consumption estimates run up to four times the dose used in that trial, so "no adverse effects at 2.4 g" does not cover six strong cups a day. And there is a published case of a woman with gestational diabetes on insulin who developed hypoglycemia after drinking raspberry leaf tea — if that is your situation, leave it alone.

If you use it anyway: tell your midwife, do not start before the third trimester, do not brew it stronger than ordinary tea, and do not expect anything measurable.

## If You Have Already Been Drinking a Lot

Most women who drank their usual tea before the test came back positive worry about this, and the answer is usually reassuring: the evidence of harm is strongest at high intakes, and concerns probability across a pregnancy, not any single cup. What is worth doing is proportionate — count your caffeine for a day or two, switch some cups to decaf or a second steep, move tea away from meals and your supplement, and mention it at your next appointment.

## FAQ

### How many cups of tea can I drink a day?

At the NHS figure of about 75 mg per mug, two to three mugs keeps you under 200 mg. At the USDA figure of 47 mg per 8 oz cup, four cups still fit — if nothing else in your day contains caffeine.

### Is decaf tea safe during pregnancy?

Yes. [Decaffeinated black tea](<>) has around 2 mg per cup, which is negligible. It still contains the polyphenols that inhibit iron absorption, so the timing rule applies.

### Can I take my prenatal vitamins with tea?

No. Separate them by about an hour. Tea polyphenols reduce absorption of the iron in your supplement, and prenatal iron is one of the few things in pregnancy where absorption really matters.

### Is chamomile tea safe?

Generally yes, in moderation, within the NHS's four-cup herbal ceiling. If you have a ragweed or daisy-family allergy, avoid it — cross-reactivity is well described.

## Key Takeaways

* **200 mg of caffeine a day** is the NHS, ACOG and EFSA limit — roughly two to three mugs, less with a big mug or coffee.
* **A second steep has about half the caffeine** of the first: the easiest way to cut intake without giving up the habit.
* **Tea with meals reduces iron absorption.** Leave an hour before iron-rich food or your prenatal supplement.
* **Licorice root is the one clear no**; laxative and "detox" teas are the products that can genuinely harm.
* **Raspberry leaf is widely used but unproven**: the UK's 2024 review found low risk with high uncertainty, and the EMA does not recommend it.
