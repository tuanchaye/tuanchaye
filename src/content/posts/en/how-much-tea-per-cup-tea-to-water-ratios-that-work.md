Most tea tastes wrong because of a number nobody hands you: how much leaf. For a standard 250 ml mug the working figure is about 3 grams — one level teaspoon of most whole-leaf black tea, two of a fluffy white. Gongfu brewing puts five to six grams into a 100 ml gaiwan, roughly five times that concentration, and steeps it for twenty seconds instead of four minutes. Almost every ratio question resolves once you know which of those two you are actually doing.

## Why Teaspoons Fail

A teaspoon measures volume. Tea is sold by weight. The gap between the two is large, because leaves differ in how much air they trap.

A level teaspoon of tightly rolled oolong weighs roughly three times as much as a level teaspoon of large-leaf white tea. Same spoon, same scoop, same gesture — a dose that varies by 300%. This is why "one teaspoon per cup" produces a bitter cup for one person and a weak one for another, both following the same instruction.

## The Ratios That Cover Everything

| Method           | Leaf per 100 ml | Typical dose             | First steep |
| ---------------- | --------------- | ------------------------ | ----------- |
| Western mug      | 1–1.5 g         | 3 g in 250 ml            | 3–5 min     |
| Grandpa style    | 1 g             | 3 g in 300 ml, topped up | 2–3 min     |
| Gongfu           | 5–6 g           | 6 g in 100–110 ml gaiwan | 10–20 s     |
| Cold brew        | 1 g             | 10 g in 1 L, fridge      | 6–12 h      |
| ISO 3103 tasting | 2 g             | 2.8 g in 140 ml          | 6 min       |

Grandpa style, for anyone who has not met it, is the Chinese habit of putting leaf straight into a tall mug, adding hot water, and topping up as you drink. No timer, no removal — the leaf stays in and the cup lightens as you go. It is the least fussy method in the table and the one most worth learning if you drink at a desk.

## ISO 3103 Is Not a Recipe

The figure you see quoted everywhere — 2 grams per 100 ml, six minutes, freshly boiling water — comes from ISO 3103, and it is not a brewing recommendation. The standard's own scope describes a method for preparing tea liquor "for use in sensory tests." It is a fixed, deliberately blunt protocol so that a Keemun tasted in London and a Darjeeling tasted in Kolkata can be judged on equal terms.

That purpose explains the numbers. Six minutes at a rolling boil extracts hard enough to expose a leaf's defects — exactly what a buyer wants, exactly what a drinker does not. The standard even specifies white porcelain apparatus, because a tinted glaze would distort the color the taster is judging.

Brew ISO 3103 at home and you get a strong, drying cup. Which is why "the official way to make tea" is a phrase worth distrusting. It is the official way to evaluate tea.

## Western and Gongfu Are One Dial, Not Two Traditions

Western brewing uses about 1.2 g of leaf per 100 ml for a single long steep. Gongfu uses about 5.5 g per 100 ml across many short ones. Both work because leaf and time trade against each other.

A fifteen-second gaiwan steep at five times the concentration extracts roughly what a four-minute mug steep does. But because the water is poured off and fresh water added, each later steep pulls out a different set of compounds. That is the appeal of gongfu — not a stronger cup, a moving one. Floral on the first steep, sweet on the third, woody by the sixth.

The ratios are not interchangeable, though. Halving the leaf in a gaiwan does not give you Western tea; it gives you weak gongfu. If you want a mug, use the mug ratio and the mug time.

## Dialing In Without a Scale

Most people do not own a scale that reads to a tenth of a gram, and for everyday brewing you do not need one. Two volume anchors work better than teaspoons:

* **Gongfu:** dry leaf should fill roughly a fifth to a quarter of the gaiwan before water goes in.
* **Western:** one loose layer of leaf covering the bottom of the mug — no gaps, no mound.

Then change one variable at a time. Adjusting leaf and time together teaches you nothing, because you cannot tell which change did the work.

表格

| What the cup tastes like             | Most likely cause                           | Change this first       |
| ------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------- | ----------------------- |
| Bitter and drying, color looks right | steeped too long                            | cut the time by a third |
| Sharp and thin at once               | water too hot for the leaf                  | drop 10 °C              |
| Watery, with a faint harsh edge      | too little leaf, steeped long to compensate | more leaf, less time    |
| Flat and dull, little aroma          | too little leaf                             | add 1–2 g               |

The third row is the one that trips people up. When a cup is weak, the instinct is to steep longer, which adds astringency without adding body. The fix is usually more leaf and less time. ## Cold Brew Has Its Own Ratio

Cold brew numbers online are wrong in a consistent way: the "optimal" figures often come from room-temperature extraction studies, not from a refrigerator. Real cold brew sits at 4 °C, and it needs both more leaf and far more time.

The workable range is 8–12 g of leaf per liter of cold water, 6–12 hours in the fridge. Green and white sit at the short end, oolong and black at the long end, and tightly rolled oolong needs the extra hours because the leaf has to unfurl before it gives anything up.

Cold water extracts caffeine and catechins slowly but pulls aromatics steadily, so cold brew comes out markedly less bitter than the same leaf brewed hot. It also lands lower on caffeine per milliliter than a hot steep of the same leaf, which matters if you drink it late — our [caffeine in tea](<>) breakdown has the ranges.

## Frequently asked questions

**How many grams of tea should I use for one cup?**About 3 g for a 250 ml mug — one level teaspoon of most whole-leaf black tea, or two teaspoons of a large-leaf white. A 350 ml mug takes 4 g.

**How many teaspoons is 5 grams of tea?**It depends entirely on the leaf. Five grams is roughly 1.5 teaspoons of rolled oolong, 2 teaspoons of twisted black tea, or 4 to 5 teaspoons of large open white leaf. This is exactly the case where a cheap 0.1 g scale earns its place.

**Does water temperature change the ratio?**The ratio stays the same, but the two interact. Hotter water extracts faster, so if you raise the temperature, shorten the steep rather than cutting the leaf. Our [tea water temperature guide](<>) has the numbers by tea type.

**Can I brew the same leaves more than once?**Yes, and gongfu is built around it. Add five to ten seconds to each successive steep and expect five or six good cups from a gaiwan. Western-brewed leaves give one acceptable second cup at most, because the first steep already took most of what was there.

## Key Takeaways

* **3 grams per 250 ml mug** is the number to remember. Gongfu uses about five times that concentration in a fifth of the water.
* **Teaspoons mislead.** A level teaspoon of rolled oolong can weigh three times a teaspoon of large white leaf, so one instruction produces very different cups.
* **ISO 3103 is a testing standard, not a recipe.** Two grams per 100 ml for six minutes is built to expose defects for buyers, which is why it tastes harsh to a drinker.
* **Leaf and time trade off only within a method.** Halving gongfu leaf does not produce Western tea; it produces weak gongfu.
* **Weak and harsh together means too little leaf**, not too little time. Add leaf before you add seconds.
