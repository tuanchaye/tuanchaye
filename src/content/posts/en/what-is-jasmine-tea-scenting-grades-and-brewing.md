Jasmine tea is green tea that has absorbed the scent of fresh jasmine blossoms. The flowers are layered over finished tea and left overnight, then removed; the tea is dried and the process repeats with a new batch of flowers. It is not tea with jasmine flavoring added, and — this surprises most people — a well-made one contains almost no flowers at all.

What you are buying is the number of nights the tea spent with flowers. That number, and the quality of the leaf underneath, is what separates a $8 tin from a $60 one.

## Scenting, Not Flavoring

The base is a finished green tea, usually a baked green from Fujian, harvested in spring and stored until summer. The flower is *Jasminum sambac*. Buds are picked in the afternoon while still closed, then kept cool until evening, because jasmine opens and releases its fragrance after dark. The whole production schedule runs on the flower's clock, not the maker's.

Once the buds open, tea and flowers are layered together and left for 10 to 12 hours. The pile warms up as the flowers respire, so it has to be broken up and spread to cool — a step called *tonghua* — then gathered again. By morning the flowers have given everything they have, and they are sifted out within about three hours. The tea is gently re-dried to drive off the moisture it picked up along with the aroma, and the next night the whole thing starts again with fresh flowers.

One round is called a *yin*. Nothing about this is fast: a top-grade jasmine tea can take six months and over a hundred separate handling steps before it is boxed.

## The Number That Sets the Price

China's national standard for jasmine tea processing, GB/T 34779-2017, specifies how many rounds and how much fresh flower each grade should receive. The spread is enormous.

表格

| Grade                         | Scenting rounds                                    | Fresh flowers per 100 kg of tea |
| ----------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------- |
| Da Bai Hao (large silver tip) | Six, plus a finishing round                        | 270 kg                          |
| Yin Hao (silver tip)          | Six, plus a finishing round                        | 200 kg                          |
| Special grade                 | Four, plus a finishing round                       | 120 kg                          |
| Grade 3                       | One pressed round, one fresh round, plus finishing | 50 kg                           |
| Grade 5                       | One pressed round, one fresh round, plus finishing | 30 kg                           |

Two terms in that table need explaining. A **pressed round** reuses flowers that have already given up most of their scent, pressed into the leaf to extract what remains — an economy measure, not a quality one. A **finishing round** (*tihua*) is a final light scenting with a small amount of the best fresh flower, around 5 to 10 kg per 100 kg of tea, done without re-baking so the top notes stay bright and volatile.

Above the standard's grades, Fuzhou makers scent premium teas up to seven or nine times, one fresh flower harvest per night. When a vendor prints "nine times scented" on the tin, that is what they are claiming — and it is a claim you can partly check by how the tea performs in a second and third infusion.

## Why There Are No Flowers in the Bag

Because they were sifted out. The flowers did their work before the tea reached you; leaving them in would only add moisture and a stale, vegetal note as they age.

Dried petals do sometimes appear in a bag, added back after the fact for appearance. A scattering is harmless. But a tea that leans on visible flowers for its appeal is often a plain base leaf with a decorative topping, and the fragrance will be gone by the second steep.

One correction to a common claim: you will read that high-grade jasmine tea must smell of jasmine alone, with no trace of other flowers. In practice the national standard explicitly permits a small amount of white magnolia (*bailan*) as a base flower — up to 1.5 kg per 100 kg of tea — to lift the jasmine. A faint magnolia undertone is normal craft, not adulteration. What you should object to is a tea that smells of magnolia *instead of* jasmine.

## Scented vs. "Jasmine Flavored"

The ingredient list settles it faster than any tasting note. "Green tea, jasmine flowers" means the tea was scented. "Green tea, natural jasmine flavor" or "jasmine flavoring" means aroma was applied, and no amount of flowers in the photograph changes that.

The steep test is more reliable than the label. Flavored tea announces itself loudly in the dry leaf and then collapses: the first cup is perfumed, the second is thin, ordinary green tea. A properly scented tea often tastes *better* on the second infusion, once the surface aroma settles and the tea's own sweetness comes through.

## What It Actually Tastes Like

The aroma compounds that transfer from flower to leaf are well characterized: methyl anthranilate, benzyl acetate, linalool, indole, methyl benzoate, and methyl salicylate. Between them they produce the grape-like, honeysuckle, and cool-floral notes people associate with the tea.

Cultivar matters more than most buyers realize. A 2025 metabolomics study compared a 'round leaf' cultivar against the traditional double-petal type through successive scenting rounds and found the round leaf more elegant and fresh, the double-petal fuller and softer. It also found the aroma saturated earlier in the round leaf — around the fourth round — while the double-petal kept improving to the fifth. Past that point, more flowers stop buying more fragrance for that particular leaf.

A word of caution about numbers you may see quoted. A 2023 metabolomics study did identify 585 compounds in scented tea against 589 in the flowers, with 70 appearing in the tea only after scenting. Those are **non-volatile** compounds — the ones responsible for taste and mouthfeel, not smell. They say nothing about aroma, and citing them as an aroma finding is a common mistake.

## Brewing It

The Fujian provincial standard for brewing and tasting jasmine tea (DB35/T 1634-2016) gives concrete parameters, and they are worth following for the vessel size and ratio.

表格

| Parameter          | Direct drinking (gaiwan or glass)  | Shared, poured into a pitcher |
| ------------------ | ---------------------------------- | ----------------------------- |
| Vessel             | 150 ml gaiwan, or 150–200 ml glass | 200–250 ml pot or gaiwan      |
| Tea-to-water ratio | 1:50 to 1:70                       | 1:30 to 1:50                  |
| Water temperature  | 90–100 °C                          | 90–100 °C                     |

The standard also says to use slightly cooler water for high-grade specialty jasmine and hotter water for standard grades — which makes sense, because the base leaf differs. A delicate bud tea scalds; a coarse graded leaf needs the heat to open up.

The infusion timing is short and cumulative:

表格

| Infusion       | Time             |
| -------------- | ---------------- |
| First          | 15–30 seconds    |
| Second         | 20–40 seconds    |
| Third          | 25–45 seconds    |
| Each one after | Add 5–10 seconds |

One thing to ignore in the standard: it also specifies that the tasting room be held at 15–27 °C with humidity no higher than 70 percent. That is a sensory-evaluation environment for professional judging, not a requirement for your kitchen. Nobody needs to climate-control a kettle.

If you are used to Western-style green tea brewing, or your jasmine is a fine bud tea, start at 80–85 °C and work upward. The baked-green base used in Fuzhou takes near-boiling water well, but cooler water is the safer first attempt. ## Storing It

Jasmine tea is the opposite of pu-erh. It does not improve with age; the aroma compounds are volatile and they leave. Keep it sealed, cool, dark, and away from anything strongly scented — tea absorbs whatever is in the cupboard, which is the whole point of how it was made. Drink it within a year. By eighteen months even a good one is flat.

Skip the refrigerator unless the tin is vacuum-sealed. Every time you open it, cold tea meets warm air and collects condensation, and moisture is the other thing that ruins it.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is jasmine tea always green tea?

No. Jasmine describes the scenting, not the tea type. Green tea is the classic and most common base, but white tea (jasmine silver needle) and black tea bases both exist and are worth trying — the same flower over a softer or maltier leaf.

### Does jasmine tea contain caffeine?

Yes, at roughly the level of the tea it is made from — typically 30 to 50 mg per cup for a green tea base, less for a white tea base. The flowers add no caffeine. Our breakdown of [caffeine content by tea type](<>) has the full range.

### Why does my jasmine tea taste bitter?

Water that is too hot, or a steep that runs long. Bitterness comes from over-extracting the leaf, and scented green tea is more fragile than it looks. Drop the water temperature by about 10 °C before you shorten the steep — you want to keep the floral top notes, and heat destroys them first.

### Is jasmine tea good for you?

The base is green tea, so it carries the same catechins and caffeine you would get from drinking that green tea on its own. The jasmine contributes aroma, not meaningful nutrition. There is animal research on jasmine tea and mood, but it has not been shown in controlled human trials, so treat it as a pleasant drink rather than a remedy. That is general information, not medical advice.

## Key Takeaways

* Jasmine tea is green tea scented with fresh *Jasminum sambac* blossoms over repeated overnight rounds — not tea with flavoring added.
* The flowers are sifted out after each round. A good jasmine tea contains almost none, and visible petals are usually decoration.
* Scenting rounds set the price: China's GB/T 34779-2017 ranges from 30 kg of fresh flower per 100 kg of tea at Grade 5 to 270 kg at the top grade.
* "Natural jasmine flavor" on the ingredient list means sprayed, not scented. Scented tea improves on the second steep; flavored tea collapses.
* Brew at a 1:50 ratio in a 150 ml vessel, 15–30 seconds for the first infusion, and drop the water to 80–85 °C if your tea turns bitter.
