Your cup of tea is about 98 percent water by weight. Everything you can taste in it — the sweetness, the grip, the aroma — comes from the other two percent. Water is not a neutral vehicle; it is an active ingredient, and its chemistry decides how much of that two percent reaches you.

Three measurements matter: total dissolved solids (TDS), hardness, and alkalinity. A fourth, pH, matters mostly because it moves with the other three. Get them roughly right and an ordinary tea tastes better than it should; get them badly wrong and a very good tea tastes like nothing.

The short version: aim for moderately soft water, near neutral, with low alkalinity. But there is a real tension underneath that advice, and it explains why two careful drinkers with equally "correct" water can disagree about what good water is.

## The Three Numbers That Matter

These are not the same measurement, and treating them as interchangeable is the most common mistake here.

表格

| Measurement    | What it actually is                                                           | What it does to tea                                                                                         |
| -------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **TDS**        | Everything dissolved — minerals, salts, organic matter — in parts per million | The headline number. Too low: thin and hollow. Too high: dull, chalky, muted aroma                          |
| **Hardness**   | Calcium and magnesium specifically, reported as calcium carbonate equivalent  | The ions that do extraction work. Too low: weak extraction. Too high: binds polyphenols, raises astringency |
| **Alkalinity** | Buffering capacity, mostly dissolved bicarbonate                              | Does not help extraction at all. Neutralizes the acids that make tea taste bright                           |
| **pH**         | Acidity on a 0–14 scale                                                       | Follows alkalinity in practice. Below 6.5 risks sourness; above 7.5 flattens the cup                        |

Hardness and alkalinity get confused constantly, and they are not the same fight. Hardness is mineral content doing work on the leaf; alkalinity is a buffer that eats the acidity in the cup. Water can show reasonable hardness and still carry enough bicarbonate to flatten every cup you brew.

## What the Tea Research Actually Found

Researchers at the Tea Research Institute of the Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences brewed light- and strong-scented [Tieguanyin oolong](<>) in four waters — purified, mountain spring, mineral, and natural — at 1:50 leaf to water for six minutes, then scored the infusions and profiled the volatiles.

表格

| Water type      | Mineralization | What the panel found                                                              |
| --------------- | -------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Mineral water   | High           | Lowest overall score. Flat taste, turbid aroma, poor leaching of flavor compounds |
| Natural water   | Moderate       | Highest total volatile compounds of the four                                      |
| Mountain spring | Moderate       | Highest overall acceptability for both oolong types                               |
| Purified water  | Near zero      | Lowest total volatile compounds of the four                                       |

Notice the shape of that result. Purified water came **last** on aroma, not first. Zero minerals is not the goal — it is one of two failure modes.

A separate 2025 study in *Food Science* tested 14 waters on raw Pu-erh, spanning pH 6.56 to 8.53 and TDS from 8 to 120 mg/L. High-TDS water increased perceived body, but excess calcium and magnesium inhibited polyphenol extraction. Near-neutral water (pH 6.5–7.5) released more amino acids and more aroma. The best-scoring liquor colors came from waters with calcium and magnesium under 5 mg/L, and aroma scores were highest where bicarbonate stayed below 80 mg/L.

The two studies do not fully agree on how soft is too soft: the oolong work found moderate mineralization beat purified water on aroma, while the Pu-erh work favored very low calcium and magnesium. But they agree on direction — more mineral means more body and less aroma; less mineral means a cleaner, brighter, thinner cup. Where to sit between them is a matter of taste and of what you brew.

## Why Hard Water Dulls Tea

High calcium does something peculiar: it lowers bitterness, freshness, and sweetness while *raising* astringency. The result is a cup that is flat and rough at once — no sweetness to round off the edges, but a drying grip on the tongue that arrives late and stays. It also suppresses the volatile compounds that carry aroma, which is why heavily mineralized water produces what the oolong study called a turbid aroma: the smell is there, but muddled, as if heard through a wall.

![](https://s.coze.cn/image/WCdSWNWbM4s/)

Here the trade-off becomes concrete. The same low-mineral water that maximizes aroma also extracts more catechin — the compounds behind bitterness and astringency. Soft water gives you a brighter, more fragrant, but also more bitter and grippier cup. Hard water gives the reverse: rounder, heavier, duller. Neither is better in the abstract; they are different drinks from the same leaf.

One caution about reading catechin numbers as health numbers: how much is extracted into the cup is not the same as how much your body absorbs. Extraction studies measure the first and cannot answer the second.

## The Thing Nobody Tells You: Boiling Changes Your Water

That Pu-erh study measured all 14 waters twice — at room temperature and after boiling. Every one changed, and all in the same direction.

* **pH rose in all 14 samples**, by 6 to 27 percent. All of them ended above 7.0. The most alkaline sample, already at pH 8.53 cold, reached 9.45 after boiling.
* **TDS rose by 4 to 200 percent.**
* **Conductivity rose by 6 to 207 percent.**

The reason is simple: dissolved carbon dioxide escapes as gas when water boils, taking carbonic acid with it, so pH climbs. More salts also dissolve at higher temperature. Your kettle is not a neutral container but a reaction vessel.

Two practical consequences. First, water measured cold is not the water you brew with; a TDS reading taken from the tap understates what is in the cup. Second, every boil drives off water as steam while leaving minerals behind. Top up the same kettle all morning without emptying it and each round starts from a higher mineral load than the last — usually why tea tastes progressively duller as the day goes on.

## Reading Your Own Water

You cannot fix what you have not measured, and measuring is cheap.

In the United States, every municipal supplier publishes an annual Consumer Confidence Report, free on the utility's website. Look for hardness, alkalinity, and which disinfectant is used; reports vary in how they present these, so read the footnotes.

A TDS pen costs about fifteen to twenty dollars. Its limit is worth understanding: it measures electrical conductivity and infers TDS, so it tells you how much is dissolved, not what. Two waters reading 150 ppm can behave completely differently, because one may be mostly bicarbonate and the other mostly calcium sulfate. Use the pen as a headline, not a diagnosis.

The most widely cited numeric reference comes from the Specialty Coffee Association: TDS 75–250 ppm with 150 as the target, hardness 50–175 ppm as calcium carbonate, alkalinity 40–70 ppm, and pH 6.5–7.5. It was built for coffee, not tea, and tea research tends to favor the lower end — but it is the only widely published standard anyone has.

## Fixing It

表格

| Problem                          | What it tastes like                      | Fix                                                                            |
| -------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Too hard (high TDS and hardness) | Chalky, dull, aroma muted                | Blend with distilled or reverse-osmosis water                                  |
| Too soft (TDS below roughly 50)  | Thin, hollow, sometimes metallic         | Blend a little tap water back in, or use a spring water with published TDS     |
| High alkalinity                  | Flat and lifeless regardless of the leaf | Carbon filters do not remove it — you need RO or dilution                      |
| Chlorine                         | Medicinal, swimming-pool edge            | Any basic carbon filter                                                        |
| Chloramine                       | The same, but harder to shift            | Standard carbon is not enough — look for catalytic carbon rated for chloramine |

Dilution is the cheapest tool and the one people forget. If your tap reads 300 ppm and you want something near 100, mix two parts tap with one part distilled. That is arithmetic, not chemistry.

Water chemistry is only one of the two variables you control at the kettle; the other is [water temperature](<>), which matters just as much.

Sequence matters more than precision: remove chlorine or chloramine first, since it is the most common problem and the cheapest to fix; measure TDS next, to see whether you are in the bad zone at all; check alkalinity third, because it quietly flattens tea while everything else looks fine; fine-tune minerals last, as the smallest lever most people reach for first.

If your water is already in a reasonable range, further tuning is fine control, not transformation: chasing a perfect number inside the good zone yields differences you will argue about rather than taste. The same is true of the leaf — no water profile rescues an ordinary tea, and [starting with leaves worth tasting clearly](<>) will change your cup more than any mineral adjustment.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the best TDS for brewing tea?

Somewhere between 50 and 150 ppm works well for most teas, though the number alone does not tell you the composition. Below about 50 ppm there are too few ions to carry flavor compounds into solution and the cup goes thin. Above roughly 250 ppm, minerals work against the leaf instead of with it.

### Does hard water ruin tea?

Not automatically. Hardness alone mainly changes body — more calcium means a heavier, rounder cup. What ruins tea is hardness combined with high alkalinity, because bicarbonate neutralizes the acids that give a cup its brightness. Water can be moderately hard and still brew beautifully if alkalinity is low.

### Can I brew tea with distilled water?

Not straight, no. Distilled and reverse-osmosis water sit near zero TDS, and the oolong study found purified water produced the lowest volatile content of the four types tested. The cup comes out clean but hollow. Use distilled to dilute hard tap water, not as brewing water on its own.

### Why does the same tea taste different at a friend's house?

Almost always the water. On a different supply, mineral load and alkalinity can differ several-fold. The same leaf brewed at 80 ppm and at 320 ppm is not really the same drink — which is also why a tea that impressed you at a vendor's counter can disappoint at home.

## Key Takeaways

* Tea is roughly 98 percent water by weight, so its mineral content is an active ingredient, not a neutral carrier.
* TDS, hardness, and alkalinity are three different measurements. Alkalinity is the one that flattens tea, and standard carbon filters do not remove it.
* More minerals means more body and less aroma; fewer minerals means a cleaner, brighter, more bitter cup. There is no single correct number, only a direction.
* Boiling raises both pH and TDS. Reboiling a partly filled kettle concentrates them further with every round.
* Fix chlorine first, then alkalinity, then fine-tune minerals. Most of the gain comes from leaving the bad zone, not chasing a perfect figure inside the good one.
