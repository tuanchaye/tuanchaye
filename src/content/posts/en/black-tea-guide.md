In the West, "black tea" means the bold, dark tea in breakfast blends. In China it is called **hong cha (红茶)** — "red tea" — named for its reddish-amber liquor rather than the dark dry leaves.

## What Makes It Black Tea?
Black tea is **fully oxidized**. After picking, the leaves are rolled to bruise them and left to oxidize until they turn coppery brown. This develops the rich, malty flavors that survive milk and sugar. Green tea, by contrast, is unoxidized and stays light.

## How to Brew It
Black tea is forgiving and easy:

- **Water temperature:** 90–100°C (just off the boil)
- **Amount:** about 3–5 g per 250 ml cup
- **Steeping:** 3–5 minutes
- It also works well brewed stronger and poured over ice, or with a splash of milk.

## Popular Chinese Black Teas
- **Keemun (祁门红茶):** elegant, wine-like, slightly cocoa notes — a classic.
- **Dian Hong (滇红):** from Yunnan, golden tips, honeyed and bold.
- **Lapsang Souchong (正山小种):** pine-smoked, distinctive, an acquired taste.

## Why People Love It
Black tea is the most approachable type for newcomers because it is robust, widely available, and hard to over-brew into something unpleasant. It also carries more caffeine than green or white tea, making it a gentle morning pick-me-up.

If you are coming from English-style tea bags, trying a whole-leaf Chinese hong cha is a revelation: smoother, sweeter, and far more aromatic than most blends.
