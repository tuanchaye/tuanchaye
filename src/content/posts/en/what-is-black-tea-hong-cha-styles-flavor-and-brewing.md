Black tea is tea that has been allowed to oxidize almost completely before drying. In Chinese it is called *hong cha* — literally "red tea" — named for the color of the brewed liquid rather than the leaf. English went the other way and named it after the dry leaf. Both names describe the same drink, and that gap causes more confusion than any other term in tea.

## Why Chinese Calls It Red Tea

*Hong cha* (红茶) is black tea. The Chinese name refers to the liquor, which ranges from amber to deep mahogany. The English name refers to the oxidized leaf, which is nearly black. Neither is wrong.

The collision comes later. What Chinese calls *hei cha* (黑茶) — literally "black tea" — is sold in English as **dark tea**, because English had already spent "black tea" on *hong cha*. A Chinese menu listing 黑茶 is offering fermented teas like pu-erh, not Keemun. Confusing the two is the most common mix-up in Chinese tea.

There is a trap the other way too. In Western shops "red tea" almost never means *hong cha* — it usually means rooibos, a South African bush with no caffeine that is not tea at all. Our guide to [true tea vs herbal tisanes](<>) draws that botanical line.

## How Black Tea Is Made

Four stages: wither, roll, oxidize, dry. The leaf is never heated to stop oxidation — that is the one step black tea omits, and it is why black tea is dark. Green tea gets its heat early and stays green; oolong gets it halfway through; black tea simply never gets it. Our walkthrough of [how tea is made](<>) covers the full sequence.

During oxidation, polyphenol oxidase converts catechins into theaflavins, which supply brightness and the brisk, slightly drying sensation on the sides of your tongue, and then into thearubigins, which supply color and body. Makers judge the endpoint by aroma: the leaf moves from cut grass to apple to malty. That takes roughly 45 minutes to three hours depending on temperature, humidity and style.

"Fully oxidized" is the textbook description, but the range is wide. Most Assam and Ceylon is taken close to completion; many Darjeelings are deliberately stopped short, which is why a first flush can look olive-green and taste closer to an oolong than to a breakfast blend.

## The Styles Worth Knowing

| **Lapsang Souchong** | Fujian, China      | large, smoke-dried over pine  | Campfire, longan, tarry; the oldest black tea            |
| **Jin Jun Mei**      | Fujian, China      | whole golden buds             | Honey, sweet potato, unsmoked                            |

| **Assam**            | Assam, India       | large-leaf *assamica*         | Strong malt, full body, takes milk well                  |
| **Darjeeling**       | West Bengal, India | small-leaf Chinese type       | First flush floral and astringent; second flush muscatel |
| **Ceylon**           | Sri Lanka          | varies by elevation           | Brisk, citrusy, bright                                   |
| **Nilgiri**          | Tamil Nadu, India  | —                             | Clean and fragrant; excellent iced                       |

**Keemun** is the refined one: small dark leaves, a wine-like body, and an aroma the Chinese call *Qimen xiang* — "Keemun fragrance" — somewhere between orchid, cocoa and dried plum. It is gentle enough to drink without milk.

**Lapsang Souchong and Jin Jun Mei come from the same village and sit at opposite ends of one tradition.** Lapsang is smoke-dried over pine fires and tastes of campfire and tar; it is the oldest style of black tea, and the smoke began as a side effect of drying leaf over open fires in the Wuyi mountains. Jin Jun Mei, created there in 2005, uses only whole buds, is never smoked, and tastes of honey and sweet potato.

**Dianhong** comes from Yunnan, grown from the same large-leaf variety used for pu-erh. The best grades are heavy with golden tips — the downy buds that oxidize to a bright orange-gold — and the cup is malty and sweet with a cocoa edge.

A note on blends: English Breakfast is a blend, not a style; Earl Grey is bergamot oil added to a black tea base.

### Why Spring-Picked Costs More

Harvest timing changes the cup. In a 2023 study of Yinghong No. 9, a large-leaf cultivar from southern China, spring leaf carried fewer astringent catechins and more amino acids — especially glutamic acid, which reads as sweetness — than summer leaf, and scored higher for taste and liquor color. That is one cultivar, so treat it as a pattern, not a law: spring picks tend to be smoother, summer picks bolder — which is why summer leaf often ends up in blends bound for milk.

## Brewing by Leaf Style

The most common mistake is brewing every black tea like a teabag — boiling water, five minutes, one cup. That works for CTC and ruins a Keemun. Whole leaf wants more leaf, less time, and several steeps.

表格

| Leaf                   | Water     | Leaf to water  | First steep    |
| ---------------------- | --------- | -------------- | -------------- |
| CTC / teabag           | 100 °C    | 1 bag / 200 ml | 2–3 min        |
| Assam, orthodox        | 95–100 °C | 3 g / 200 ml   | 3 min          |
| Keemun, Jin Jun Mei    | 90–95 °C  | 5 g / 150 ml   | 20–30 s        |
| Dianhong               | 90–95 °C  | 5 g / 150 ml   | 10–15 s        |
| Lapsang Souchong       | 95 °C     | 4 g / 200 ml   | 2–3 min        |
| Darjeeling first flush | 85–90 °C  | 3 g / 200 ml   | 3 min, no milk |

Three rules cover most of it. Bitterness is not strength — it is extracted tannin, produced by time and heat rather than by quantity of leaf. Whole leaf rewards short repeated steeps: add five to ten seconds each round and the same leaves give five or six cups. And milk is a style decision, not a habit: Assam and CTC stand up to it, while Keemun and first-flush Darjeeling are buried by it. ## Does Black Tea Actually Help Your Health?

Most tea writing oversells this. The honest version: the associations are real, modest and observational.

The largest study on black tea specifically followed 498,043 British adults aged 40 to 69 for a median of 11.2 years; 29,783 died. Those drinking two or more cups a day had a 9% to 13% lower risk of death from any cause than non-drinkers, with similar inverse associations for cardiovascular mortality, ischemic heart disease and stroke — whether or not they added milk or sugar, and regardless of caffeine-metabolism genetics.

A separate Chinese cohort of roughly 486,000 adults followed for about nine years found habitual tea drinkers had around 11% lower risk of cardiovascular disease overall.

Then the detail that matters day to day: in that same cohort, people who drank their tea scalding hot had roughly **16% higher** cardiovascular risk than non-drinkers — the benefit disappeared and reversed. Drinks above 65 °C are classified as probably carcinogenic to the esophagus, and the mechanism is simple repeated thermal injury. Let a fresh cup sit two or three minutes. Temperature is the variable you control; the polyphenols are not.

None of this is a reason to start drinking tea, or to stop. Cohort studies compare people who already differ in a hundred ways; a 9% difference across a population says nothing certain about your next cup.

## Storing It

Black tea is more forgiving than green tea — fully oxidized, holding little moisture — but not shelf-stable forever. Airtight, opaque, cool and dry, away from coffee and spices, it holds its character for two to three years. It will not improve with age; only dark teas do that. ## Frequently asked questions

**Is black tea the same as red tea?**Yes, if you mean *hong cha*. But if an English-language shop sells you "red tea," you are almost certainly buying rooibos — an herbal infusion unrelated to *Camellia sinensis*.

**Does black tea have more caffeine than green tea?**Not inherently. Dry leaf from the same plant carries similar caffeine; the bigger variables are leaf part and shade growing, which is why matcha outruns most black teas. What shifts the number is how you brew: black tea is usually made hotter, longer and with more leaf, so the cup often lands higher than a lightly brewed green.

**What is the difference between black tea and dark tea?**Processing. Black tea is oxidized by its own enzymes, with no microorganisms involved. Dark tea, including pu-erh, is pile-fermented by molds and bacteria after drying — the only true microbial fermentation in tea. In Chinese, black tea is 红茶 and dark tea is 黑茶.

**Can you drink black tea with milk?**Yes, and for Assam or a CTC teabag it is the intended way to drink it. Milk flattens the aromatics that make Keemun and first-flush Darjeeling worth their price, so try those plain first.

## Key Takeaways

* **Black tea is fully oxidized leaf.** *Hong cha* means "red tea" and names the liquor; English names the leaf. Same drink, two vantage points.
* **"Black tea" in Chinese (黑茶) is not this tea.** That is dark tea, sold under that name in English, and it is microbially fermented rather than enzymatically oxidized.
* **Style matters more than the category.** Keemun is floral and wine-like, Lapsang smoky, Dianhong malty, Darjeeling first flush closer to oolong than to a breakfast blend.
* **Brew by leaf, not by habit.** Whole leaf wants 90–95 °C, plenty of leaf, and 10–30 second steeps; teabags want boiling water and three minutes.
* **The health evidence is real but modest and observational** — and it reverses if you drink your tea scalding hot.
