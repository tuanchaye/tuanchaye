Moroccan mint tea — *atay* in Moroccan Arabic — is Chinese gunpowder green tea boiled hard with a fistful of fresh spearmint and more sugar than most foreign palates expect, then poured from a height into small glasses until a foam forms on the surface. It is drunk several times a day, offered to every guest, and served in three glasses from the same pot.

It is also considerably younger than most visitors assume — it reached Morocco in the mid-nineteenth century, younger than the American Civil War.

## What Atay Is, and What It Isn't

The definition is narrow: Chinese gunpowder green tea, fresh spearmint of the *nana* variety, sugar, boiling water, and a particular method of rinsing, boiling and pouring.

It does not mean any tea with mint. Peppermint (*Mentha × piperita*) is not the mint used here. The mint is spearmint, *Mentha spicata*, sold in bunches in every Moroccan market as *nana*.

The tea is gunpowder, the tightly rolled pellet style from Zhejiang province — the same style covered in our guide to [choosing loose-leaf green tea](<>). The pellet form matters: it survives long storage without losing aroma, it can be boiled repeatedly without disintegrating, and it releases slowly enough to stand up to sugar and mint.

## A National Drink Younger Than the American Civil War

Morocco grows no tea. Its own beverage traditions ran to *qishr* (brewed from coffee husks), rosewater sherbets and, later, European coffee.

The most repeated account begins with a diplomatic gift: Anne of Great Britain is said to have sent chests of Chinese green tea to Sultan Moulay Ismail in thanks for the release of English prisoners. That story appears in most histories and cannot be verified. What is documented is that tea stayed a court curiosity for roughly a century.

In 1854, during the Crimean War, a British naval blockade closed the Baltic ports that had been the main market for British tea merchants — the same trade that built [British tea culture](<>). Ships carrying Chinese gunpowder were redirected to the Atlantic coast of North Africa and unloaded at Tangier and at Mogador, today's Essaouira. From there, merchants — including a large Jewish trading community in Essaouira — carried tea and sugar inland.

Within a generation it moved from coastal merchants to the imperial court and from the cities into the Atlas. By the late nineteenth century the court employed an official *mūl atay*, a master of tea; by the early twentieth, atay was a national practice.

Two things explain its grip. The first is that the imported tea was cheap and harsh — high in tannin and not immediately liked. Moroccans already infused mint, sage, wormwood and verbena, and already imported sugar. Adding both to a bitter green tea turned a difficult import into a daily drink.

The second is social: atay fills a role alcohol fills elsewhere — offered at the start of a visit, during a negotiation, in a shop, after a meal, at a wake. It is how a transaction becomes a relationship.

## The Three Glasses

Atay is served three times from the same pot, with hot water and sugar added between rounds. One glass is a discourtesy; two is a hurry; three is a visit.

A well-known Maghrebi saying is attached to the three glasses:

> The first glass is bitter as life, the second sweet as love, the third gentle as death.

The Arabic is الكأس الأولى مرّة كالحياة، والثانية حلوة كالحبّ، والثالثة لطيفة كالموت — literally, the first cup is bitter as life, the second sweet as love, and the third gentle as death.

English versions of it contradict each other. Plenty of guidebooks print the line as "gentle as life, strong as love, bitter as death" — the exact reverse. The Arabic above is the version given in collections that reproduce the original text; reversed English orders are usually a translation error copied onward.

The saying, though, is poetry rather than a tasting note, and its physics is disputed. One reading holds that the glasses weaken as the leaves give out: the first, brewed shortest, is most astringent; the second is sweetest; the third faint and herbal. The other points out that the host does not brew three separate pots — the same pot is topped up, so later glasses are usually stronger.

What is not in dispute is the ritual's shape. The saying is about time and welcome, not extraction. A host who serves one glass has said you are welcome as far as the bitter goes; a host who serves three has shown you the whole arc.

## How It Is Made

There is no single authorized recipe, but the sequence is consistent.

| 1. Rinse   | Two tablespoons of gunpowder go into the empty pot; about 100 ml of boiling water follows; it stands one minute, then is poured off into a glass and kept                                         | Called the "spirit" of the tea — the clear essence before the leaves open |
| 2. Wash    | A second glass of boiling water is added and the pot is swirled hard; the water turns dark and cloudy and is thrown away                                                                          | Removes dust and the worst bitterness                                     |
| 3. Rebuild | The reserved "spirit" goes back in; the pot is filled with boiling water; sugar goes in (three to four tablespoons for a standard pot, more by preference); then a large bunch of fresh spearmint | The mint stems are twisted and broken by hand first, to release the oils  |
| 4. Boil    | The pot goes directly onto low heat until the liquid rises toward the spout                                                                                                                       | Called *sheher*; it fuses mint and tea and lightly caramelizes the sugar  |
| 5. Mix     | A glass is poured out and immediately poured back, two or three times                                                                                                                             | Distributes sugar and mint evenly                                         |
| 6. Pour    | The pot is lifted and the tea poured from 30–50 cm above the glass in one unbroken stream                                                                                                         | Aerates, cools, and builds the foam                                       |

Scale roughly: 10 g of gunpowder for a 300–400 ml pot, a bunch of mint about the same volume as the dry tea, and sugar at a level most non-Moroccan drinkers find extreme.

## The Pour

The high pour is the part visitors remember, and it is not decoration. It aerates the liquid, softening the tannic edge and opening the aroma; it cools a tea that has just come off the stove to something drinkable in the time the stream spends in the air; and it builds the foam — *el rizza* — a crown of fine bubbles on the surface of each glass.

In Morocco, tea without foam is regarded as badly made, which is why the host pours in front of the guests.

The technique: start with the spout close to the glass, then lift steadily, keeping the thread unbroken. Just before the glass is full, drop the pot quickly to cut the stream cleanly.

## What the Etiquette Actually Means

The rules are simple, and they are about time.

Refusing the first glass is normal and expected — a polite demurral; the host pours anyway. Refusing the third is not. Accepting and then leaving immediately is worse than either.

Guests drink slowly, say something appreciative (*bnin* — delicious), and stay. In a shop, tea signals that bargaining has begun in good faith. In a meeting, it comes before the agenda. At a wake, it is what the family offers so that people will remain.

None of this is ceremonial in the museum sense. It is a working social mechanism.

## One Drink, Many Versions

The base is national; the details are not.

* **North (Tangier, Tetouan, Chefchaouen):** lighter tea, more mint, sometimes a drop of orange blossom water, reflecting Andalusian influence.
* **Middle and High Atlas:** wormwood (*chiba*), sage or thyme in winter, when mint is scarce.
* **Sahara and Tuareg communities:** less mint, more tea, brewed slowly over embers — the version closest to what travelers call Saharan tea.
* **Urban Casablanca and Rabat:** less sugar than a generation ago, sometimes a stainless pot on a gas ring rather than silver over charcoal.

All of these are atay. There is no single correct version, and Moroccans will argue about which household makes it best — much as a [Chinese tea ceremony](<>) is argued over regionally, for entirely different reasons.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Moroccan mint tea the same as green tea with mint?

No. The base must be Chinese gunpowder green and the mint must be fresh spearmint (*nana*). The method — rinsing, boiling on the stove, pouring from height — is part of the definition.

### Why do Moroccans pour tea from such a height?

Three reasons: it aerates the tea and softens the tannins, it cools a just-boiled liquid to drinking temperature, and it creates the foam (*el rizza*) that signals a well-made pot.

### How much sugar goes into a pot?

Traditionally a great deal — three to four tablespoons for a standard pot, often more. Sugar is not optional: it makes a harsh, high-tannin gunpowder drinkable several times a day.

### Is Moroccan mint tea recognized by UNESCO?

Not on the official list. Morocco has sixteen elements inscribed on UNESCO's Intangible Cultural Heritage lists, most recently the caftan in 2025 and henna in 2024. Mint tea is not among them, despite what several travel sites state. The confusion seems to come from 2021, when Morocco's inscriptions were *tbourida*, falconry and Arabic calligraphy.

## Key Takeaways

* Atay is a specific drink: Chinese gunpowder green tea, fresh spearmint (*Mentha spicata*), sugar, and a boil-and-pour method. It is not "any tea with mint."
* It arrived in the mid-nineteenth century, when British merchants redirected Chinese tea to Tangier and Essaouira during the Crimean War — younger than the American Civil War.
* The three glasses are the unit of hospitality: one is a discourtesy, two is a hurry, three is a visit. The famous saying about life, love and death exists in several contradictory English translations.
* Morocco grows no commercial tea. The entire national drink rests on imported Chinese gunpowder.
