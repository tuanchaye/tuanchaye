Caffeine is what decides how tea affects your sleep, not L-theanine. A standard 200 ml cup of true tea carries roughly 8–25 mg of L-theanine — about a tenth of the dose used in the sleep trials — alongside 25–70 mg of caffeine, a molecule with a half-life of around five hours. The theanine is real. The dose is simply too small to outvote the caffeine in the same cup. So the useful questions are not "is tea calming?" but "which leaf, how much, and how late?"

## The Two Compounds That Decide the Outcome

Caffeine works by blocking adenosine receptors, the docking points your brain uses to build up sleep pressure. It does not create energy; it hides tiredness. It also nudges your body clock: in a controlled inpatient study, a caffeine dose equivalent to a double espresso taken three hours before habitual bedtime delayed the evening melatonin rhythm by about 40 minutes — roughly half the delay produced by three hours of bright evening light. Only five people took part, so treat the size of the effect as an estimate; the direction has held up.

Timing matters more than any single figure. In a randomized, double-blind crossover trial, 400 mg of caffeine taken immediately before bed, three hours before and six hours before all significantly disturbed sleep compared with placebo, and the authors concluded that substantial caffeine should be avoided for at least six hours before bedtime. Note the dose: 400 mg is roughly two large coffees or four to six strong mugs of tea.

L-theanine is the amino acid that makes tea feel calmer than coffee at the same caffeine level. A 2025 meta-analysis pooling around 900 participants found small-to-moderate improvements in self-reported sleep quality, with one limitation: many included trials used multi-ingredient blends rather than pure L-theanine. The trials that did isolate it used 200–450 mg per day, with 200 mg before bed a common protocol.

Here is the part most tea articles skip. A standard 200 ml cup of green or black tea delivers only about 8–25 mg of L-theanine. To reach a single 200 mg dose from tea alone you would need eight to twenty-five cups — and with them, several hundred milligrams of caffeine. L-theanine contributes to how tea feels; it is not a sleep aid in the clinical sense.

## Which Teas Are Actually Lower in Caffeine

Caffeine is set mainly by which part of the plant goes into the package, how it was grown, and how you brew it — not by how dark the finished leaf is.

**Leaf position.** The young bud and first two leaves are the most caffeine-rich parts of the plant, because caffeine is a natural insecticide concentrated in new growth. An all-buds silver needle can hit harder than a large-leaf oolong. Stems contain very little, which is why kukicha — literally "stem tea" — is Japan's traditional low-caffeine choice.

**Shading.** Matcha and gyokuro are covered for two to four weeks before picking, and the plant responds with more theanine *and* more caffeine. Shade-grown is not the gentle option people assume; a bowl of matcha often lands near a cup of filter coffee.

**Roasting and oxidation.** These change aroma, color and polyphenols far more than caffeine. Roasted hōjicha is low in caffeine because it is made from mature leaves and stems, not because it was roasted, and black tea (hong cha) is not higher in caffeine than green tea from the same leaf grade — only stronger in flavor, as our guide to the [six families of tea](<>) explains.

**Brewing** is the lever you control for free. Caffeine is highly water-soluble, and extraction climbs with time and temperature — the same variables behind most [tea brewing mistakes](<>). Halving the leaf and cutting the steep from four minutes to ninety seconds does more than switching from black to green.

## What the Research Shows About Tea and Sleep

Observational data on tea and sleep is thinner and messier than the confident headlines suggest, and it points two ways at once.

The negative signal comes from a cross-sectional study of 104 Indonesian adults aged 18–64 with low stress scores. Daily tea intake correlated with poorer sleep quality on the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (Spearman's r = 0.53, p < 0.001), with about 40% drinking three or more cups a day and 72% classified as poor sleepers. That is a moderate correlation in a small sample, and a cross-sectional design cannot establish which came first.

The positive signal comes from a 2024 *Frontiers in Sleep* study of midlife women in Mexico City combining surveys with interviews. Unsweetened tea intake was associated with longer sleep duration, and the women described herbal teas as their main home remedy for sleeplessness. The association is observational, and what they described is a cultural practice — a warm, unsweetened, caffeine-free drink at the end of the day — not a pharmacological effect of a particular leaf.

Read together, the two are compatible. Caffeinated tea drunk through the evening tracks with worse sleep; a warm unsweetened cup used as a wind-down ritual tracks with better sleep. What separates them is not tea but caffeine and context.

## Do Herbal Teas Help? What the Evidence Supports

**Chamomile** has the largest evidence base of any bedtime infusion, and it is more modest than the marketing. A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis pooled ten trials with 772 participants and found a significant improvement in Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index scores (weighted mean difference −1.88 points, 95% CI −3.46 to −0.31). Sleep onset improved in three of the four trials that measured it, and night-time awakenings in two of three. But total sleep duration, sleep efficiency and daytime functioning did not improve, heterogeneity was very high, and only one of the ten studies checked whether blinding held and whether the product matched its label.

The fair reading: chamomile may help you fall asleep and stay asleep a little, by self-report, in specific groups such as postpartum women and older adults. It is not a treatment for insomnia.

**Green tea extract** is a cautionary story. In a mice study, chronic administration of green tea ethanol extract delayed sleep latency and increased wakefulness for several hours, effects the authors attributed mainly to its concentrated caffeine load. That is animal data at supplement doses and says nothing about a cup of tea — except that "green tea" stops being calming once the dose is concentrated.

**Matcha** has one small randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial in healthy Japanese adults. Four weeks of daily matcha providing roughly 50 mg of L-theanine and 72 mg of caffeine did not change objective sleep on EEG, yet participants felt more satisfied with their sleep. Read that as a dosing story: 72 mg of caffeine taken earlier in the day is low enough not to register on an EEG, while the theanine and the ritual may explain the subjective lift.

## Building an Evening Tea Habit That Actually Works

Do the arithmetic once and the decisions get easy. Caffeine's half-life averages about five hours, ranging from roughly 1.5 to 9.5 hours depending on genetics, smoking, pregnancy and liver function. A 60 mg cup at 4 p.m. still leaves about 30 mg circulating at 9 p.m., and 15 mg at 2 a.m.

* **Set a cutoff, not a limit.** Six hours before bed is the minimum for most people, eight is safer if you are sensitive. If bedtime is 11 p.m., your last caffeinated cup belongs at 3–5 p.m.
* **Change the leaf before the habit.** Hōjicha, kukicha and lightly brewed white tea sit at the low end; keep matcha and strong black tea for the morning.
* **Brew weaker on purpose.** Less leaf, cooler water, shorter steep — ninety seconds instead of four minutes can halve the caffeine without halving the ritual.
* **Count the whole day.** Most health authorities put the ceiling for healthy adults at 400 mg a day, and three strong mugs before noon plus one at 6 p.m. can approach it.
* **Keep the ritual.** If what you want at 10 p.m. is a warm drink and ten quiet minutes, a caffeine-free infusion delivers that. The chemistry is not the only active ingredient.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does drinking tea before bed ruin my sleep?**Not automatically — it depends on dose and timing. 400 mg still disturbed sleep six hours before bed; a 30 mg cup three hours before is a far smaller exposure. Move your last caffeinated cup earlier rather than give up tea.

**Is the L-theanine in tea enough to help me sleep?**No. A cup contains roughly 8–25 mg, while trials showing sleep benefits used 200–450 mg a day. Reaching 200 mg from tea would take eight to twenty-five cups, and the caffeine would work against you.

**What is the best tea to drink before bed?**Anything caffeine-free: chamomile, rooibos, roasted barley, lemon balm. If you want true tea, choose hōjicha, kukicha or lightly brewed white tea, use less leaf, and finish it three hours before bed.

**Does decaf tea still keep you awake?**Decaf retains under 5 mg per cup, which is unlikely to matter for most people, and decaf green tea keeps some L-theanine — but decaf is reduced, not eliminated.

## Key Takeaways

* **Caffeine sets the ceiling.** A cup carries 25–70 mg of caffeine against 8–25 mg of L-theanine — roughly a tenth of the trial dose. The caffeine decides your night.
* **Timing beats type.** 400 mg still disturbed sleep six hours before bed. Set a cutoff six to eight hours out.
* **Leaf part, not color, drives caffeine.** Buds and shade-grown leaves are highest; stems and mature leaves lowest.
* **Brewing is the free lever.** Halving the leaf and steeping ninety seconds instead of four minutes can halve the caffeine.
* **Chamomile is modest, not magic.** Trials show better self-reported sleep quality and fewer awakenings, but no gain in duration or efficiency.
