Tie Guan Yin (铁观音, "Iron Goddess of Mercy") is arguably the most famous oolong in the world — and a perfect example of why Chinese tea captivates people.

## The Story
Named after Guan Yin, the Buddhist goddess of mercy, the tea comes from Anxi county in Fujian. The name "iron" refers to the tightly rolled, heavy balls of leaf that unfold into surprisingly large, fragrant leaves.

## Flavor Profile
A well-made Tie Guan Yin is:
- **Floral** — orchid-like, sometimes described as "lan xiang"
- **Creamy and smooth**
- **Sweet with a clean finish**

Modern styles range from **lightly roasted** (brighter, greener, more floral) to **lightly roasted/traditional** (warmer, deeper). Both are legitimate; taste to prefer.

## How to Brew
Use a **gaiwan or small pot**, 5–7 g leaf, water **90–95°C**, and short steeps of 15–30 seconds, repeated 5–7 times. The aroma peaks in the first few infusions.

## Why It Matters
Tie Guan Yin is the oolong that wins many people over to Chinese tea — approachable yet complex, fragrant yet not perfumed. If you try only one oolong, make it this one.

A note: buy from a source that states origin and harvest; "Tie Guan Yin" is widely imitated, and freshness matters enormously.
