If you are new to Chinese tea, the single most useful idea to learn is that all tea comes from the same plant — *Camellia sinensis* — and what makes teas different is **how the leaves are processed**, especially how much they are oxidized.

Chinese tea is traditionally divided into six major types:

## 1. Green Tea (绿茶)
Unoxidized. The leaves are heated right after picking to stop oxidation, keeping them green and fresh. Flavor: grassy, vegetal, light. Examples: Longjing (Dragon Well), Biluochun.

## 2. White Tea (白茶)
The least processed. Leaves are simply withered and dried. Flavor: delicate, sweet, floral. Ages beautifully. Example: Bai Hao Yin Zhen (Silver Needle).

## 3. Yellow Tea (黄茶)
Rare and similar to green tea, but with a gentle "sealing yellow" step that softens the taste. Mellow and smooth.

## 4. Oolong Tea (乌龙茶)
Partially oxidized — somewhere between green and black. Huge aroma range, from floral to roasted. Examples: Tie Guan Yin, Da Hong Pao.

## 5. Black Tea (红茶)
Fully oxidized. In Chinese it is called *hong cha* ("red tea") because of the reddish liquor. Bold, malty, often great with milk. Example: Keemun, Dian Hong.

## 6. Dark Tea / Post-fermented (黑茶)
Fermented by microbes and often aged. Earthy and smooth. The most famous is Pu-erh from Yunnan.

## Which Should You Start With?
If you like light, fresh flavors, begin with **green tea**. If you prefer something rounder and sweeter, try **oolong** or **black tea**. White tea is the gentlest entry point of all.

Understanding these six types gives you a map for the entire world of Chinese tea. From here, you can explore regions, seasons, and craftsmanship with confidence.
