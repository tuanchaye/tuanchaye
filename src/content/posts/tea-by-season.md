In China it is common to drink tea by the season — not as a rule, but as a gentle rhythm that matches weather and mood. Here is a practical way to follow it.

## Spring: Fresh Greens
As new buds appear, light **green and yellow teas** celebrate the season's freshness. Their grassy, lively character feels right after winter.

## Summer: Cooling Whites and Greens
Hot weather calls for **white tea and cold-brewed green tea** — light, hydrating, and low in caffeine. Iced or room-temperature, they refresh without weighing you down.

## Autumn: Aromatic Oolongs
As the air turns dry, **medium-roast oolongs** (Tie Guan Yin, Dan Cong) offer comforting aroma and body — a bridge between the light and dark seasons.

## Winter: Warming Reds and Pu-erh
Cold months suit **black tea (hong cha)** and **ripe pu-erh** — bold, warming, easy on the stomach after heavy meals.

## The Real Point
This calendar is a suggestion, not a law. The deeper idea is *attention*: notice how a tea fits the day's temperature, your energy, and your meal. A steaming cup of ripe pu-erh on a winter evening is its own kind of comfort.

Start by simply rotating one tea type per season and see how your preference shifts. You may discover you are already a seasonal drinker.
