Both England and China love tea — but the two cultures approach it in almost opposite ways. Understanding the difference makes both more enjoyable.

## The Leaf
- **China:** drinks many types — green, oolong, white, pu-erh — usually unblended, whole leaf, often no milk.
- **England:** built its tradition on **black tea**, frequently in blends like English Breakfast, often with milk and sugar.

## The Brewing
- **China:** small vessels (gaiwan, clay pot), high leaf-to-water ratio, **many short infusions** from the same leaves. The focus is on the leaf's changing character.
- **England:** a large pot, one long steep, poured into big cups, often with additions.

## The Ritual
- **China:** tea is about **presence and hospitality** — offering a cup is a gesture of respect; the gongfu style turns brewing into a calm, attentive practice.
- **England:** tea is about **occasion and comfort** — afternoon tea with scones, a daily pause, a social anchor.

## Which Is "Better"?
Neither. They serve different needs. Chinese style invites you to *taste* the tea; English style invites you to *relax* with it. Many modern drinkers enjoy both: a gaiwan of oolong in the morning, a mug of milky breakfast tea in the afternoon.

If you grew up with English tea, trying a Chinese session (small pot, short steeps) is a revelation — the same leaves reveal layers you never get from one long brew.
